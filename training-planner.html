📘 Quick chess lessons

Short lessons with examples and questions to try. Read each pattern first, then choose your answer.

LESSON 1 · OPENING

Develop your pieces

In the opening, bring your knights and bishops into the game, fight for the center, and get your king safe by castling. Try not to move the same piece again and again without a good reason.

Example: 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4. White develops a knight and bishop. The knight attacks the e5-pawn, and the bishop looks toward f7.

Try it: After 1. e4 e5, which move develops a knight and attacks e5?

LESSON 2 · TACTICS

Spot a knight fork

A fork is one move that attacks two or more targets at the same time. Knights are excellent fork-makers because they can jump over other pieces.

Example: Imagine a white knight on d5, a black king on c7, and a black rook on f6. The knight on d5 checks the king and also attacks the rook. That double attack is a fork.

Try it: A knight checks the king while attacking a rook. What tactic is this?

LESSON 3 · CHECKMATE

Understand back-rank mate

A back-rank mate happens when a rook or queen gives check along the king's home row and the king has no safe square to escape.

Example: A black king is on g8 with its own pawns on f7, g7, and h7. A white rook on e8 checks along the eighth rank. The rook controls f8 and h8, while the black pawns block the king's way down. If no black piece can block or capture the rook, it is checkmate.

Try it: In this example, why can't the black king escape to the seventh rank?

LESSON 4 · KING SAFETY

Castle to protect your king

Castling is a special move that moves your king and one rook in the same turn. It helps tuck your king behind pawns and brings the rook closer to the centre. For a short castle, White's king moves from e1 to g1 and the rook moves from h1 to f1. For a long castle, the king moves from e1 to c1 and the rook from a1 to d1.

Short-castle example:

Before: White's king is on e1, the rook is on h1, and f1 and g1 are empty.

After O-O: The king is on g1 and the rook is on f1.

Three safety checks: The king and chosen rook have never moved; every square between them is empty; and the king is not in check, does not cross an attacked square, and does not finish on an attacked square.

Try it: A bishop attacks White's rook on h1. The king on e1 and the squares f1 and g1 are safe, neither piece has moved, and the path is empty. Can White castle short?

LESSON 5 · TACTICS

Recognize an absolute pin

A pin happens when moving an attacked piece would expose something valuable behind it. In an absolute pin, the valuable target is the king, so the pinned piece cannot legally move if that would leave its king in check.

Pattern: White bishop on g5, Black knight on f6, and Black king on e7. The bishop, knight, and king are lined up on the diagonal g5–f6–e7. The knight blocks the bishop's attack on the king. If the knight moves away and opens that diagonal, the bishop gives check.

Compare: If the black queen were on e7 instead of the king, the knight would be in a relative pin. It could legally move, but the bishop would then attack the queen. A pinned piece is not always completely stuck—work out exactly what is behind it.

Try it 1: In the pattern above, why can't the black knight move away?

Try it 2: If the piece behind the knight on e7 were a queen instead of a king, what kind of pin would it be?

LESSON 6 · TACTICS

Reveal a discovered attack

Sometimes one of your pieces stands in the way of a rook, bishop, or queen. When that piece moves, the long-range piece behind it can attack a target. This is called a discovered attack.

Simplified line pattern (not a full game position): Your rook is on d1, your knight is on d4, and the enemy queen is on d8. The other squares on the d-file are empty. Your knight blocks the rook's path. If the knight moves to a safe square, the rook's line opens and attacks the queen. The knight may create a second threat too. The queen is not automatically lost—always calculate the opponent's reply.

Try it 1: A knight moves away and reveals a rook attacking the enemy queen. What tactic is this?

Try it 2: What is it called when the newly opened rook line gives check to the enemy king?

LESSON 7 · TACTICS

Win material with a skewer

A skewer is a line attack on two pieces. The more valuable piece—often the king—is attacked first and must move, exposing the piece behind it to attack. Think of it as a pin in reverse.

Simplified pattern (only the pieces named are shown): White has a rook on a1, a knight on d6, and a king on g1. Black has a king on a6 and a queen on a8. The a-file squares between the rook and king are clear. The rook checks the king, with the queen lined up behind it. The knight on d6 controls b5 and b7, so the black king cannot escape there; a5 and a7 are on the rook's line. In this simple position, the king can move to b6. Then the rook's line opens and White plays Rxa8, winning the queen.

Try it 1: Which piece must move first to answer the rook's check?

Try it 2: After the black king moves to b6, what can White play to win the queen?

LESSON 8 · TACTICS

Deflect a defender

A deflection is a tactic that pulls an enemy piece away from an important job, such as guarding a rook or protecting a key square. A forcing check, capture, or threat may make the defender leave. A decoy is related: it lures a piece onto a particular square, often an awkward one.

Simplified pattern (not a complete board position): A black queen on d8 is the only defender of a black rook on d7. A white rook on d1 is lined up with the rook on d7, but capturing it now would allow the queen to recapture. If White finds a forcing move that makes the queen leave d8 and stop protecting d7, White may then capture with Rxd7. Before doing so, check whether another black piece protects the rook and calculate the queen's best reply.

Try it 1: What does a deflection try to do?

Try it 2: The queen leaves d8, but a black bishop also guards the rook on d7. Is Rxd7 automatically safe?

LESSON 9 · TACTICS

Use a decoy to lure a piece

A decoy is a move that tempts an enemy piece onto a particular square, where a follow-up tactic can work. The target is often the king. A deflection is related, but its main idea is to pull a defender away from an important job.

Worked pattern (only the listed pieces are shown; assume all unlisted squares are empty):

White: queen b1, knight d5, bishop c3. Black: king g8, queen d7, pawns f7 and h7.

1. Qxh7+! White's queen captures the h7-pawn and checks the king on g8. In this pattern, h7 is not protected by another White piece, so Black can capture with 1...Kxh7. The black pawn on f7 blocks the black queen's path along the seventh rank, so it cannot simply recapture on h7.

2. Nf6+! The knight checks the king on h7 and attacks the queen on d7 at the same time. White's bishop on c3 protects f6, so the king cannot capture the checking knight. After the king moves, White can play Nxd7 and win the queen.

Lesson: The queen sacrifice was a decoy: it drew the king onto h7, where a knight fork became possible. In a real game, check every legal reply before sacrificing.

Try it 1: What is the main idea of a decoy?

Try it 2: In the example, why can't the black king capture the knight after 2. Nf6+?

⚡ Zwischenzug puzzles · Intermediate moves

Zwischenzug means “in-between move.” Before making an obvious recapture, pause and look for a forcing check, threat, or attack. These short training scenarios practise the decision-making pattern.

PUZZLE 1 · CHECK FIRST

Can you interrupt the recapture?

Position story: Your opponent has just captured one of your pieces. You can recapture it, but you also have a legal queen check that attacks the enemy king and may win material.

Your task: What should you calculate before automatically recapturing?

PUZZLE 2 · CREATE A THREAT

Attack before you take back

Position story: A piece has been exchanged and a recapture is available. You notice a move that attacks the opponent's queen with tempo. The queen must move, giving you time to improve the position before deciding whether to recapture.

Your task: Which move best fits the idea of an in-between move?

PUZZLE 3 · CALCULATE THE ENDING

When should you finally recapture?

Position story: You found an intermediate check. After the opponent answers, the original target is still available—but the board has changed. One of your pieces may now be attacked.

Your task: What is the most important next step after the opponent answers your check?

Training note: These are concept-based decision puzzles, not engine-verified board positions. On a real board, always confirm that the check or threat is legal and that your material remains safe.
